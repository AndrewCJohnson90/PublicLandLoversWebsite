# The Border Wall Comes to Big Bend

Big Bend is one of the wildest corners of the United States.

Here, the Rio Grande winds through a landscape of desert, mountains, canyons, and grasslands. Big Bend National Park, Big Bend Ranch State Park, and thousands of acres of other public lands protect a landscape that feels remarkably distant from the rest of the country.

It is also part of the U.S.–Mexico border.

Now, a new generation of border infrastructure is moving into this landscape.

The proposed border barrier system would bring miles of new walls, roads, lighting, staging areas, and other infrastructure into the Big Bend region—potentially changing some of the most remote landscapes in Texas.

The question isn't whether the United States should secure its border.

The question is **how we secure it without unnecessarily sacrificing the landscapes, wildlife, and public lands along the way.**

## What Is Being Proposed?

The federal government has identified the Big Bend region as part of its broader effort to expand and strengthen border infrastructure along the U.S.–Mexico border.

The projects can involve much more than the physical wall itself.

A modern border barrier system can include:

- Border barriers and gates
- Patrol roads
- Access roads and staging areas
- Lighting
- Cameras and surveillance infrastructure
- Drainage and erosion-control structures
- Construction areas and equipment access
- Other supporting infrastructure

In Big Bend, the geography makes this particularly complicated.

The border does not follow a flat, straight line across an empty landscape.

In places, the Rio Grande forms the international boundary. Mountains rise directly from the river. Canyons cut through the desert. Roads are few, terrain is rugged, and some areas are accessible only by long stretches of remote dirt roads.

Building infrastructure here means building it **through the landscape itself**.

## Why Big Bend Is Different

We've spent time traveling through Big Bend, and one of the things that stands out is just how enormous the landscape feels.

There are places where you can drive for hours without seeing another vehicle.

There are mountains where the nearest paved road is miles away.

And there are stretches of the Rio Grande where the river itself is the only obvious sign that an international boundary exists.

That remoteness is part of what makes Big Bend special.

It is also part of what makes border infrastructure so complicated here.

A wall doesn't simply occupy the few feet where it is constructed.

It requires access.

It requires roads.

It requires construction corridors.

It requires maintenance.

And in a landscape where natural drainage patterns, wildlife movement, and human access are all tightly connected to the terrain, those changes can extend well beyond the footprint of the barrier itself.

## A Landscape of Public Lands

The Big Bend region contains an extraordinary concentration of public and protected lands.

The largest is **Big Bend National Park**, which protects more than 800,000 acres along the Rio Grande.

Nearby, **Big Bend Ranch State Park** protects another roughly 300,000 acres of rugged desert landscape.

The region also includes Bureau of Land Management lands, state lands, wildlife habitat, conservation areas, and privately held lands.

Together, these lands create a connected landscape that supports wildlife and protects one of the largest remaining examples of the Chihuahuan Desert in the United States.

That connectivity matters.

A wildlife corridor doesn't stop being a wildlife corridor because a boundary happens to run through it.

And a desert ecosystem doesn't recognize an international border.

---

## Wildlife Doesn't Recognize the Border

The Big Bend region is home to an extraordinary variety of wildlife.

Black bears, mountain lions, bobcats, javelina, mule deer, desert bighorn sheep, bats, birds, reptiles, and countless smaller species move through the region.

Some species cross the Rio Grande regularly.

Others move through the broader landscape in search of food, water, mates, or seasonal habitat.

For wildlife, the border is simply part of the landscape.

A continuous barrier can change that.

Research on border fencing throughout the Southwest has documented the potential for barriers to restrict wildlife movement, fragment habitat, and reduce access to important resources.

The effects vary considerably by species.

A fence that presents little challenge to a bird may be a major obstacle for a tortoise or other ground-dwelling animal.

For large mammals, the height, design, and placement of a barrier can determine whether an animal can cross at all.

In a landscape as large and interconnected as Big Bend, those differences matter.

---

## The Rio Grande Is More Than a Border

The Rio Grande is one of the defining features of Big Bend.

It is also an ecological corridor.

Along much of the river, the vegetation changes dramatically from the surrounding desert. Cottonwoods, willows, shrubs, and other riparian vegetation provide habitat in an otherwise arid landscape.

The river also shapes the way people experience Big Bend.

Canoeing through Santa Elena Canyon.

Hiking along the river.

Looking across the water toward Mexico.

Watching the river wind between the mountains.

These experiences depend on a landscape that remains relatively intact.

Infrastructure near the river therefore has implications beyond the structure itself.

Construction can disturb soils and vegetation, alter access, and introduce roads and other features into areas where very little permanent infrastructure currently exists.

And in a desert environment, disturbed ground can take a long time to recover.

## What Happens When the Rules Are Waived?

One of the most unusual aspects of the current border infrastructure program is the role of federal waivers.

The federal government has the authority under the **REAL ID Act of 2005** to waive certain laws in order to expedite construction of border infrastructure.

Those waivers have been used extensively during recent border barrier projects.

In Big Bend, the issue is particularly significant because the normal environmental review process is designed to identify exactly the kinds of landscape-level effects that make this region complicated.

Environmental review can require agencies to consider things such as:

- Wildlife habitat
- Threatened and endangered species
- Cultural resources
- Water resources
- Wilderness and other protected lands
- Recreation
- Scenic values
- Soil and vegetation impacts
- Cumulative effects

When those requirements are waived, much of that process can be reduced or bypassed.

That doesn't mean environmental impacts disappear.

It means there may be fewer opportunities to identify them, compare alternatives, modify project designs, or require mitigation before construction begins.

And that distinction is important.

**A waiver changes the process. It doesn't change the landscape.**

## A Complicated Landscape Needs a Careful Process

There is a reasonable argument to be made that border security is an important federal responsibility.

There is also a reasonable argument that infrastructure built in some of the most remote and environmentally sensitive parts of the country deserves careful planning.

Those ideas don't have to conflict.

The problem is what happens when we treat a complicated landscape as though it were simple.

Big Bend is not an empty strip of land waiting for infrastructure.

It is a functioning ecosystem.

It is habitat.

It is a river corridor.

It is a national park.

It is a state park.

It is wilderness.

It is wildlife habitat.

It is a cultural landscape.

And it is a place that millions of dollars and decades of conservation work have gone into protecting.

That doesn't mean infrastructure can never be built here.

It means **where and how it is built matters.**

## What Happens Next?

Border infrastructure in Big Bend is an evolving issue.

Project locations, designs, construction schedules, environmental requirements, and federal policies can change.

That makes it especially important to look beyond a single announcement or headline.

We will continue updating this map as new information becomes available and as projects move through planning and construction.

If you care about Big Bend, the best thing you can do is stay informed.

Read the underlying documents.

Follow the agencies managing the lands.

Learn about the places affected.

And pay attention when opportunities for public input or legal review become available.

Public lands are difficult to protect once something has been built.

Planning matters.

---

## The Bigger Question

The debate over border infrastructure is often framed as a choice between security and conservation.

We don't think it has to be.

America can secure its borders.

And America can protect the extraordinary public lands that happen to sit along them.

But doing both requires acknowledging that landscapes are complicated.

The Rio Grande doesn't care where the boundary is drawn.

Wildlife doesn't recognize a national park boundary.

Water doesn't follow political lines.

And ecosystems don't come with convenient construction corridors.

The Big Bend landscape has been here for thousands of years.

We should be thoughtful about what we choose to build into it.

> **Border security matters. So do the public lands that make up the border. The challenge is finding a way to protect both.**
