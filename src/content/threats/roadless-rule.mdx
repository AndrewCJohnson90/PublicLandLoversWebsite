Some of the wildest places in America don't have a national park sign.

They don't have a famous entrance road.

There may not even be a road.

Instead, they are pieces of national forest where the landscape has remained relatively intact—places where forests stretch across mountain slopes, rivers begin in remote headwaters, and wildlife can move without crossing a maze of roads.

For more than two decades, many of those places have had an additional layer of protection.

It is called the **Roadless Area Conservation Rule**.

More commonly known as the **Roadless Rule**, the 2001 rule limits road construction, road reconstruction, and commercial timber harvesting across approximately **58.5 million acres of inventoried roadless areas on National Forest System lands**. That is roughly 30% of the National Forest System.

Now, that protection is on the chopping block.

In August 2026, the U.S. Forest Service proposed rescinding the nationwide Roadless Rule.

The proposal would return much of the decision-making authority for these areas to individual national forests and their existing land-management plans.

The rule has not been repealed yet.

But the process has begun.

And if the proposal is finalized, one of the country's largest nationwide protections for undeveloped national forest lands will disappear.

The Roadless Rule doesn't protect every roadless acre in America.

It applies specifically to **inventoried roadless areas on National Forest System lands**.

But those areas make up an enormous network of undeveloped public forests.

And the map shows why what happens to them matters far beyond the boundaries of an individual national forest.

## What Is the Roadless Rule?

The Roadless Rule was adopted by the U.S. Forest Service in 2001 after years of public debate over how to manage some of the country's remaining undeveloped national forest lands.

The basic idea was straightforward.

Some forests are valuable precisely because they haven't been developed.

Building a road into a remote forest can make other activities possible.

Once a road exists, timber can be hauled out.

Mining equipment can get in.

Recreation becomes more accessible.

Development can follow.

And a landscape that once functioned as a relatively intact ecosystem becomes connected to a much larger network of human infrastructure.

The Roadless Rule established national restrictions on road construction, road reconstruction, and timber harvesting in inventoried roadless areas, with specific exceptions.

It did not create wilderness.

It did not close these lands to the public.

And it did not prohibit every form of management.

People can still hike, hunt, fish, camp, ski, ride, climb, and explore many roadless areas.

The rule was designed to preserve something much simpler:

**the absence of roads and the ecological and social benefits that come with it.**

## Why Roads Matter

A road is never just a road.

It is a physical connection between a remote landscape and the outside world.

Roads can make forests easier to access for recreation, firefighting, timber harvest, mining, and other activities.

They can also fragment habitat, alter drainage, increase erosion, introduce invasive species, and provide access for human-caused wildfire ignitions.

The ecological effects depend heavily on where a road is built and what happens around it.

A road through a previously isolated valley can have very different consequences from a short administrative road in an already developed area.

That is one of the reasons roadless areas matter.

They provide large blocks of habitat where roads and associated development are limited.

In a country where road networks reach deep into many forests, those remaining roadless landscapes are increasingly uncommon.

## What Does the Proposed Repeal Actually Do?

This is where the issue can become confusing.

The proposed repeal **does not mean 58.5 million acres will immediately be logged or covered with roads.**

The Forest Service itself makes that distinction.

If the rule is rescinded, roadless areas would continue to be governed by the applicable forest plans for each national forest.

Future projects would still have to be consistent with those plans and comply with applicable environmental and other laws.

Specific road construction or timber projects would still require site-specific decisions and review.

So what changes?

The national prohibition goes away.

Instead of having a nationwide rule restricting these activities, decisions would increasingly be made through individual forest planning and project-level processes.

That means the immediate effect is not a bulldozer.

It is a **change in the rules governing what can be proposed and considered.**

And that distinction matters.

## Why the Forest Service Wants to Repeal It

The Forest Service and Department of Agriculture argue that the Roadless Rule has become a barrier to active forest management.

The agency says local forest managers are better positioned to understand the conditions on individual national forests and determine where roads, timber harvest, thinning, and other management activities are appropriate.

The administration has also connected the proposal to wildfire risk.

The argument is that decades of fire suppression, insect outbreaks, disease, and changing forest conditions have created forests with unhealthy fuel loads in some places.

Giving local managers greater flexibility, the Forest Service argues, could make it easier to reduce those risks and improve forest health.

There is a legitimate management question here.

Forests are not static.

Wildfire is a natural ecological process in many western forests.

Some forests have been heavily altered by fire suppression, insects, disease, climate change, and past management.

There are places where active forest management may make sense.

But that doesn't necessarily answer the larger question:

**Does opening the country's remaining roadless forests to more roads and timber development actually solve the wildfire problem?**

## Roads and Wildfire

This is one of the most contested parts of the debate.

The administration argues that restrictions on roads and timber harvest can make it harder for managers to access forests and reduce hazardous fuels.

Opponents argue that roads can create their own problems.

Roads bring people into previously remote landscapes.

And people are responsible for the vast majority of wildfire ignitions in the United States.

Research and recent analyses have found relationships between roads, human access, and wildfire occurrence, although the relationship varies substantially by region and landscape.

That makes the wildfire argument more complicated than simply saying:

**More roads = less fire.**

Wildfire risk depends on fuels, weather, topography, climate, ignition sources, vegetation, and how a forest is managed over time.

There are places where roads can improve access for firefighters.

There are also places where roads can increase human access and ignition risk.

The right answer is likely to be different from one forest to another.

Which raises an important question about the proposed repeal:

**Should the decision be made nationally, locally, or somewhere in between?**

## Water Starts in the Mountains

Roadless forests aren't valuable only because they look wild.

They are also important places where water begins.

Many inventoried roadless areas sit high in mountain watersheds.

Snow accumulates in these forests.

Rain falls across them.

Water moves through soils and vegetation before entering streams and rivers.

Those streams eventually supply communities, farms, ecosystems, and major watersheds downstream.

The Forest Service itself identifies watershed protection as one of the important values associated with roadless areas.

In the Northern Region alone, the Forest Service notes that inventoried roadless areas occur across hundreds of major watersheds.

Road construction can alter that system.

Cutting into a mountainside can change drainage.

Dirt roads can erode.

Sediment can enter streams.

Culverts can fail.

And roads can create long-term maintenance requirements in places that were previously difficult to access.

None of those impacts necessarily make a road unacceptable.

But they are reasons why **where the road goes matters.**

## Wildlife Needs More Than Protected Islands

Roadless areas are also important because of their size.

A large block of relatively intact habitat can provide something that smaller protected areas cannot:

**space.**

Large forests can support animals that require extensive home ranges.

They can provide migration routes.

They can connect different ecosystems.

They can give species room to move as conditions change.

And they can provide refuges from development and human disturbance.

The importance of that connectivity becomes even greater as climate and vegetation patterns change.

A species may not be able to survive in the same place forever.

It may need to move upslope.

Northward.

Into a different watershed.

Across a mountain range.

A network of relatively intact public lands can make those movements possible.

Roads and development can break that connectivity apart.

## Old Forests and Rare Species

Roadless areas are not necessarily untouched forests.

Many have been shaped by fire, insects, disease, Indigenous stewardship, historic logging, and other natural and human influences.

But some contain old-growth and mature forests that are increasingly rare.

Those forests provide complex habitat structures that younger forests may not have.

Large trees.

Standing dead trees.

Downed wood.

Multi-layered canopies.

Cool, shaded streams.

These features support a wide range of species.

The proposed repeal has therefore raised concerns about impacts to species that depend on mature or relatively undisturbed forest habitat.

That includes species ranging from salmon and trout to northern spotted owls, grizzly bears, wolves, and many less-famous plants and animals.

Not every roadless area contains threatened or endangered species.

Not every road would harm them.

But when large blocks of habitat are opened to a broader range of development possibilities, the cumulative effects can matter.

## The Tongass Is a Different Kind of Roadless Forest

One of the most important parts of the national roadless story is Alaska's **Tongass National Forest**.

The Tongass is the largest national forest in the United States and contains enormous expanses of temperate rainforest.

It is also one of the world's largest remaining intact temperate rainforest ecosystems.

The Tongass has had a complicated relationship with the Roadless Rule.

In 2020, the Trump administration removed the Tongass from the rule.

The Biden administration restored those protections in 2023.

The current 2026 proposal would once again rescind the nationwide rule, including its protections for the Tongass.

The Tongass demonstrates why the word "roadless" can mean something very different depending on where you are.

In southeastern Alaska, roadless forests include enormous watersheds, old-growth rainforest, salmon habitat, and landscapes that are connected to local economies and Indigenous communities.

A road through this landscape isn't simply a transportation project.

It can become a permanent connection into a remote ecosystem.

## Roadless Doesn't Mean Off Limits

One misconception about the Roadless Rule is that it closes these lands to people.

It doesn't.

Roadless areas remain public lands.

People can recreate in them.

Hunters can hunt.

Anglers can fish.

Hikers can explore.

Backcountry skiers can travel.

Horseback riders can ride.

Scientists can study them.

And communities can continue to use surrounding national forests.

The distinction is between **access to the landscape and development of the landscape.**

A person can walk into a forest without needing a road.

A road, by contrast, changes the physical character of the place.

It can make the landscape more accessible—but it can also make it easier to extract resources from it.

That tension is at the center of the Roadless Rule debate.

## The Argument Over Local Control

Perhaps the most important philosophical question in the proposed repeal is who should make these decisions.

The current Roadless Rule establishes a national baseline.

The proposed repeal would shift more authority back to individual national forests and the forest planning process.

The Forest Service argues that local managers know their landscapes better.

That makes intuitive sense.

A forest in Alaska is not a forest in Colorado.

A forest in Montana is not a forest in North Carolina.

Wildfire, timber markets, recreation, wildlife, water resources, and local economies all vary dramatically.

But national rules exist for a reason too.

Some public-land values extend far beyond a single forest.

Clean water flows downstream.

Wildlife crosses forest boundaries.

Salmon migrate through multiple jurisdictions.

Air and water don't stop at a ranger district.

And the loss of one roadless area may not seem significant until hundreds of individual decisions begin to accumulate across a landscape.

The debate is therefore not simply about federal versus local control.

It is about **how much protection should be guaranteed nationally before local decisions begin.**

## A Landscape-Level Question

This is where the Roadless Rule fits into a much larger conversation about public lands.

Environmental decisions are often made one project at a time.

One road.

One timber sale.

One mine.

One trail.

One fuel-reduction project.

Each project may seem reasonable on its own.

But landscapes don't experience projects individually.

They experience them together.

A new road connects to an old road.

A timber sale changes a forest next to another timber sale.

A development project adds another piece to an increasingly fragmented landscape.

The cumulative effect can be much larger than any individual project.

That is why maps matter.

They allow us to step back from individual projects and see the landscape as a whole.

## What Happens Next?

As of August 2026, the Roadless Rule has **not yet been repealed**.

The Forest Service has proposed rescinding the 2001 Roadless Rule and is conducting an environmental review and public comment process.

The current comment period is scheduled to run through **September 21, 2026**.

If the proposal is finalized, the national restrictions on road construction, road reconstruction, and timber harvesting in inventoried roadless areas would be removed.

That would not automatically authorize a road or timber sale on every roadless acre.

Future projects would still be governed by individual forest plans and applicable environmental laws.

But the national backstop would be gone.

That means what happens next could increasingly depend on decisions made forest by forest.

For people who care about these lands, that makes paying attention more important—not less.

## The Bigger Question

The Roadless Rule debate is often framed as a choice between conservation and forest management.

We don't think it has to be.

Forests need management.

Wildfire is real.

Insects and disease are real.

Rural communities depend on forests.

Timber is a legitimate use of public land.

Firefighters need access to some places.

But roadless forests provide something too.

Clean water.

Wildlife habitat.

Recreation.

Carbon storage.

Old forests.

Large connected ecosystems.

And simply the opportunity to experience a public landscape without roads running through it.

The question isn't whether forests should ever be managed.

It's **which places are worth keeping roadless.**

Once a road is built, it is very difficult to make the landscape truly roadless again.

Once a forest is fragmented, restoring that connectivity can take generations.

And once a remote place becomes accessible to development, it can be difficult to put the genie back in the bottle.

America's national forests belong to all of us.

That doesn't mean every acre should be locked away.

It does mean that some places are valuable precisely because they remain wild.

**The challenge is deciding which places we need to keep that way.**

### Roadless Rule Background

The 2001 Roadless Area Conservation Rule established restrictions on road construction, road reconstruction, and timber harvesting across approximately 58.5 million acres of inventoried roadless areas on National Forest System lands.

The Forest Service describes these areas as comprising approximately 30% of the National Forest System.

### 2026 Proposed Repeal

The Forest Service's 2026 proposal would rescind the national Roadless Rule and return primary decision-making authority to the forest planning process and local Forest Service officials.

The agency states that future road and timber projects would still need to comply with applicable forest plans, NEPA, and other laws.

The current public comment period is scheduled to close September 21, 2026.
