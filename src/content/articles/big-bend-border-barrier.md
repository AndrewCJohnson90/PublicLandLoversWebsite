Border security matters.

That should not be a controversial statement, and it does not need to be for the debate over what is happening along the southern boundary of Big Bend National Park.

The United States has a responsibility to control its borders, respond to illegal crossings and drug trafficking, and protect the people who live and work in border communities. Border Patrol agents also have a difficult job, often operating in some of the most remote and unforgiving terrain in the country.

But securing a border does not mean every mile of that border should be treated the same.

Few places make that distinction more important than Big Bend.

Big Bend National Park contains more than 800,000 acres of mountains, desert, canyons, river corridors, and some of the largest remaining stretches of wild landscape in the contiguous United States. Along its southern boundary, the Rio Grande forms a natural international boundary through a landscape where cliffs, mountains, canyons, and rugged desert already make movement difficult.

That complexity matters.

When infrastructure is proposed in a landscape like this, the question should not simply be *Can we build it?*

It should be:

> **Do we need it here? Where do we need it? What is the least destructive way to accomplish the objective? And what will we lose by putting it there?**

Those are precisely the kinds of questions that environmental planning and review processes are designed to answer.

## The Numbers Matter

The debate over border infrastructure in Big Bend is often framed around the idea that the region is an important corridor for illegal entry.

There is a reason for that framing: Border Patrol does encounter people attempting to cross the border in the Big Bend Sector.

But the scale and geography of that activity deserve closer examination.

Data compiled by [Keep Big Bend Wild](https://keepbigbendwild.org/big-bend-border-statistics/) from National Park Service and Border Patrol statistics show a dramatic difference between the number of people apprehended within Big Bend National Park and the much larger numbers recorded across the Southwest border.

According to the organization's compilation, there were **200 apprehensions inside Big Bend National Park in 2025**. Its table compares that figure with **443,671 apprehensions and encounters across the Southwest Border**, putting activity inside the park at approximately **0.045% of the regional total**. The same dataset reports 3,096 apprehensions in the Big Bend Sector, which includes far more territory than the national park.

These numbers require an important caveat. Keep Big Bend Wild notes that its National Park Service figures are reported by calendar year while Border Patrol figures are reported by fiscal year, and that the NPS data were unpublished and provided informally. The organization also notes assumptions involved in some of the north-of-park figures.

Separately, CBP's FY2025 data show 3,096 apprehensions in the Big Bend Sector out of 237,538 across the U.S.-Mexico border, or about **1.3% of the national total**. That represents a 74% decline from the sector's FY2023 level.

The distinction is important because the Big Bend Sector is enormous.

The sector is responsible for approximately **517 miles of the U.S.-Mexico border**, while Big Bend National Park contains about **118 miles** of that boundary.

Those miles are not interchangeable.

The 517-mile sector includes vast areas outside the national park. Statistics for the entire sector should not automatically be used to describe activity inside the park itself.

That is precisely why geography matters.

These numbers do not mean illegal crossings are irrelevant.

They mean the landscape requires context.

## A Border Is Not Just a Line on a Map

Maps can make the border look deceptively simple.

Draw a line along the Rio Grande. Put infrastructure beside it. Connect the roads. Add barriers, cameras, sensors, and patrol routes.

But the ground tells a different story.

The border in Big Bend crosses steep mountains, deep canyons, rocky desert, riparian corridors, and terrain where simply reaching the river can require substantial travel.

That means a border-security strategy designed for flat or heavily developed portions of the Southwest cannot simply be copied into Big Bend.

The landscape itself is part of the security equation.

In much of the Big Bend region, rugged terrain already prevents or severely restricts vehicle travel. Keep Big Bend Wild argues that border infrastructure should account for those natural barriers rather than constructing new roads into country where the terrain itself already provides a substantial obstacle.

That is not an argument against security.

It is an argument for designing security around the landscape.

## This Is Why Environmental Review Exists

This is where the current controversy becomes bigger than a debate over walls.

In 2026, the Department of Homeland Security issued waivers covering numerous federal laws in order to expedite construction of border barriers and roads in the Big Bend region.

The June 9 Federal Register notice states that DHS determined there was an "acute and immediate need" for additional physical barriers and roads in the project area. It then waived a broad collection of federal, state, and local legal requirements associated with several federal statutes, including the National Environmental Policy Act, Endangered Species Act, Clean Water Act, National Historic Preservation Act, and Migratory Bird Treaty Act.

That may make construction faster.

It does not necessarily make the resulting project better.

Environmental review is often criticized as an obstacle to getting things done. But at its best, environmental planning is not about stopping development. It is about understanding a place before permanently changing it.

It forces basic questions:

* Where are the sensitive habitats?
* Where are archaeological sites?
* Where does water move through the landscape?
* Where are endangered species found?
* Where will roads cause erosion?
* Which existing roads can be used?
* Which routes would create the least disturbance?
* Where does the terrain itself already provide a barrier?
* Can cameras, sensors, thermal imaging, or other technologies accomplish the same security objective without clearing miles of new roads?

And perhaps most importantly:

> **Is the proposed infrastructure actually necessary at the proposed location?**

Those questions become particularly important when the landscape being modified is a national park.

Big Bend was not established simply because the land was empty. It was protected precisely because the landscape has exceptional ecological, scenic, geological, cultural, and recreational value.

When the normal review process is removed, decision-makers lose one of the principal mechanisms for answering those questions before construction begins.

## Construction Began While the Public Was Still Being Asked to Comment

The timing of the work is one of the most troubling aspects of the current situation.

CBP opened a public comment period for its Big Bend Vehicle Border Barrier System, known as BBT-5. The project covers approximately 157 non-contiguous miles in Brewster, Terrell, and Val Verde counties, with comments due August 31, 2026.

Meanwhile, heavy equipment was already moving ground inside Big Bend National Park.

That creates a fundamental problem with public participation.

A comment period is supposed to provide an opportunity for the public to influence decisions before those decisions are implemented—not simply to comment while irreversible work is already taking place nearby.

The problem is particularly acute when the work involves roads and clearing.

Once vegetation has been removed and a road corridor has been cut into the desert, the landscape cannot simply be returned to its previous condition with the stroke of a pen.

## "Survey and Design Work"

When images of heavy equipment began circulating, the nature of the work became a subject of intense public scrutiny.

On August 13, CBP Commissioner Rodney Scott described the activity as **"survey and design work."** He also said CBP was building one new access road, improving existing roads, installing detection technology, and placing vehicle barriers in limited, strategic locations.

But satellite imagery analyzed by Bellingcat showed **more than five miles of new road construction** inside Big Bend National Park.

The imagery showed new roads extending through riparian forest and floodplain areas near the Rio Grande, including locations near Cottonwood Campground, Santa Elena Canyon, and Mariscal Canyon.

Whatever terminology is used to describe the work, the physical reality is what matters.

More than five miles of new road had been cut into the landscape.

Surveying a landscape and permanently clearing a road corridor through it are not equivalent activities.

And that distinction matters because physical disturbance creates consequences of its own.

A landscape cannot be un-cleared.

A road cannot simply be put back the way it was.

Once heavy equipment has cut across a desert slope, the consequences can extend well beyond the width of the road itself through erosion, altered drainage, habitat fragmentation, invasive species, and changes to the character of an otherwise undeveloped landscape.

## Build Less. Use What Already Exists.

There is a reasonable middle ground between doing nothing and building an entirely new transportation network along the border.

It starts with using the infrastructure that already exists.

Big Bend National Park already has an extensive network of roads, including roads that require substantial maintenance. Building new roads through undisturbed country while existing roads have deferred maintenance deserves serious scrutiny.

Where an existing road can meet an operational need, improving that road should generally be preferable to clearing a new corridor.

Where a significant natural barrier already exists, that barrier should be incorporated into the design.

And where terrain makes a new road impractical, technology should be considered before a bulldozer.

Modern border surveillance does not require a wall on every mile.

Cameras, wireless sensors, infrared and thermal systems, and other technologies can provide information without necessarily creating continuous physical barriers through some of the most sensitive portions of the landscape.

In fact, CBP's own description of the project includes detection technology and sensors alongside roads and vehicle barriers.

The technology exists.

The question is whether it can be used more extensively in places where physical infrastructure would cause disproportionate damage.

## Big Bend Deserves Site-Specific Planning

Perhaps the most important lesson from this controversy is that infrastructure decisions cannot be made effectively from a map alone.

Big Bend needs site-specific planning.

A road that makes sense in one portion of the border may be completely unnecessary a few miles away. A vehicle barrier that makes sense across flat desert may accomplish little at the base of a mountain. A surveillance system that requires a road in one location may be replaced by a remote sensor in another.

Consider Mariscal Mountain.

A road constructed across extremely steep and rugged terrain simply to maintain a border alignment deserves a much higher burden of justification.

If the terrain itself makes the road difficult to construct, difficult to maintain, and incompatible with the operational requirements of the vehicles expected to use it, then the logical question is why the road needs to exist at all.

The answer should come from engineering, operations, ecology, and land-management analysis—not from the assumption that every mile of border must look the same.

## Security and Conservation Are Not Opposites

There is a tendency in debates over public lands to divide people into two camps.

Those who want security.

And those who want conservation.

Big Bend demonstrates why that division is too simplistic.

A person can believe strongly in border security and still believe that a national park should not be unnecessarily bulldozed.

A Border Patrol agent can need reliable access without needing a new road in every location.

A conservationist can support surveillance technology that makes the border safer while opposing infrastructure that permanently damages sensitive habitat.

And a taxpayer can support spending money on border security while questioning why those dollars are being used to create new roads when existing roads already require maintenance.

These positions are not contradictory.

They are what responsible planning looks like.

## The Question We Should Be Asking

The question is not whether the United States should secure its border.

It should.

The question is whether we are willing to secure it intelligently.

Big Bend is a landscape where the costs of getting the answer wrong are unusually high. The mountains, canyons, desert, river, wildlife habitat, archaeological resources, and wilderness character cannot simply be reconstructed after a project is finished.

That is why environmental laws and planning processes exist.

They are not guarantees that development will never occur. They are safeguards intended to ensure that when development does occur, decision-makers understand what they are changing and have considered alternatives before the bulldozers arrive.

In Big Bend, those safeguards were largely removed through federal waivers intended to expedite construction.

Now the public is being asked to respond to infrastructure while ground disturbance has already begun.

That is backwards.

The answer should not be to abandon border security.

It should be to slow down, look closely at the landscape, use the tools and infrastructure we already have, and determine where additional infrastructure is truly necessary.

Secure the border.

Protect the park.

And recognize that in a place as complicated and extraordinary as Big Bend, those goals should not have to be enemies.
