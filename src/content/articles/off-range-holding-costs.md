*Wild horses and burros have become icons of the American West. But across millions of acres of public land, their numbers have grown far beyond the levels managers determined the landscape can sustain. The result is an ecological and economic problem that gets harder—and more expensive—to solve every year.*

There is something undeniably beautiful about seeing wild horses on public land.

We've had those moments ourselves.

You're driving down a lonely dirt road, miles from the nearest town, and suddenly there they are. A band of horses standing against a mountain range. Dust hanging in the evening light. A foal keeping close to its mother.

It's easy to understand why people love them.

Wild horses have become one of the most recognizable symbols of the American West—a living connection to the history and independence that we've come to associate with these landscapes.

But spend enough time on public lands and you start to notice another side of the story.

Follow those horses to a spring.

Look at the ground around the water.

Look at the trails converging from every direction. The vegetation cropped short. The soil churned into bare ground. The streambank worn down by thousands of hooves.

Then look beyond the horses.

Look for the sage-grouse.

Look at the meadow.

Look at the water.

The question becomes less about whether we like horses.

The question becomes:

> **How many horses can the landscape sustain?**

---

## A Number That Keeps Growing

The Bureau of Land Management manages the vast majority of America's free-roaming horses and burros. The U.S. Forest Service also manages designated wild horse and burro territories on National Forest System lands.

Both agencies operate under the Wild Free-Roaming Horses and Burros Act of 1971.

The law famously describes these animals as "living symbols of the historic and pioneer spirit of the West."

But the same law also directs the federal government to manage them in a way that maintains a *thriving natural ecological balance* on public lands.

That second part is becoming increasingly difficult.

As of March 1, 2026, the BLM estimated that **85,466 wild horses and burros were living on the range**. The maximum nationwide Appropriate Management Level, or AML, was **25,592 animals**.[^1]

That's more than three times the maximum level the agency has determined can be maintained in balance with other resources and uses.

And those numbers don't include the animals that have already been removed.

As of May 2026, another **58,025 horses and burros were being held in off-range facilities**, including short-term corrals and long-term pastures.[^1]

Tens of thousands of animals are living on public lands.

Tens of thousands more are living in facilities paid for by the public.

And every year, more are born.

The scale of the problem is difficult to comprehend.

## The West Is a Landscape of Scarcity

The American West can look enormous.

From the windshield of a van, it can feel almost infinite.

But ecologically, much of it is anything but abundant.

Rainfall can be measured in inches. Summer temperatures can climb into triple digits. A spring may be the only reliable source of water for miles.

And in an otherwise dry landscape, a green meadow can be an oasis.

Those places matter.

They capture and hold water. They provide forage. They support insects, birds, mammals, and countless other species. They stabilize streambanks and provide some of the most productive habitat available in an otherwise harsh environment.

They also attract horses.

A 2021 study of ten spring-fed meadows in Nevada found that free-roaming horses selected these meadows at an average rate **51 times greater than the surrounding rangeland**.[^2]

The horses didn't simply pass through.

They stayed.

They grazed intensely.

And they returned.

The study found that horses used most of the meadows for longer periods and grazed them more intensely than cattle or domestic sheep. The researchers also found that longer and more intensive use was associated with increased soil alteration and bare ground.[^2]

These weren't just random patches of grass.

The study sites were spring-fed meadows where BLM horse management areas or Forest Service territories overlapped with public-land grazing allotments and **core greater sage-grouse habitat**.[^2]

Where the meadows didn't have enough time to recover, riparian ecosystem functions became impaired.

That's an important distinction.

The problem isn't simply that horses eat grass.

The problem is **where they concentrate, how intensely they use those areas, and whether the landscape has enough time to recover.**

## The Meadow Matters

If you've spent time in sagebrush country, you've probably noticed how dramatically the landscape changes around water.

A dry hillside can suddenly become green.

Sagebrush gives way to willows, grasses, sedges, and wildflowers.

Birds appear.

Insects appear.

Wildlife appears.

For sage-grouse, those productive areas can be particularly important during parts of their life cycle.

Greater sage-grouse are sagebrush obligates, but they don't spend every stage of their lives eating sagebrush. Their habitat needs change throughout the year, and wet meadows and other productive seasonal habitats can provide important forage and brood-rearing resources.

So when we talk about horses and sage-grouse, the story isn't that horses are eating sagebrush.

It's what happens to the **places surrounding the sagebrush**.

The same Nevada study that documented intense horse use of spring-fed meadows examined sites overlapping core sage-grouse habitat. The researchers found that prolonged and intensive horse use was associated with soil alteration, increased bare ground, and impaired riparian function.[^2]

But that still leaves an important question.

Does that habitat pressure actually translate into fewer sage-grouse?

Research from Peter Coates and his colleagues suggests that it can.

In 2021, Coates and his team analyzed 15 years of sage-grouse population data and examined the relationship between horse abundance and sage-grouse population dynamics.

They found that for every **50 percent increase in horse abundance above the maximum AML, sage-grouse abundance was predicted to decline by approximately 2.6 percent annually**.[^3]

That's not the same thing as saying horses are responsible for every sage-grouse decline.

They aren't.

Wildfire matters. Drought matters. Precipitation matters. Habitat loss and fragmentation matter. Changes in sagebrush cover matter.

Sage-grouse are dealing with a long list of pressures.

The important finding was that horse abundance appeared to be **another measurable pressure**.[^3]

Then researchers began looking more closely at what might be happening to individual birds.

---

## From Habitat to Survival

In 2024, Jeffrey Beck, Peter Coates, and their colleagues examined survival across multiple stages of the sage-grouse life cycle, including nests, broods, and juveniles.[^4]

The study used 15 years of data from central Wyoming and included nearly 1,100 nests along with hundreds of broods and juveniles.

The results were striking.

When horse abundance increased from **100 percent to 300 percent of maximum AML**, predicted survival declined by:

* **8.1% for nests**
* **18.3% for early broods**
* **18.2% for late broods**
* **18.2% for juveniles**[^4]

A horse doesn't have to eat a sage-grouse to affect a sage-grouse.

It can alter the habitat around water.

It can intensively use the most productive parts of the landscape.

It can change vegetation and soil conditions.

And those changes can eventually show up in something as consequential as whether a nest survives or a chick makes it through its first weeks of life.

That's what makes this issue so complicated.

The impact isn't always dramatic enough to see in a single photograph.

Sometimes it shows up as a percentage in a scientific paper.

## In the Desert, Water Becomes the Battlefield

Farther south, the landscape changes.

The sagebrush gives way to creosote, cactus, rocky mountains, and open desert.

Here, the animal at the center of the story isn't the sage-grouse.

It's the desert tortoise.

Agassiz's desert tortoise has evolved for an environment where food and water are scarce. It spends much of its life underground, emerging to forage when conditions are right.

It is also dealing with an extraordinary number of threats.

Habitat loss. Invasive plants. Wildfire. Roads. Development. Recreation. Predators. Disease.

And feral burros.

A study by Kristin Berry, Julie Yee, and Lisa Lyren examined desert tortoise presence across the western Sonoran Desert and evaluated 12 potential influences.

Feral burro presence and vegetation association emerged as the two strongest predictors in their analysis. The researchers found a **negative association between feral burro presence and desert tortoise presence**.[^5]

That finding deserves some nuance.

It doesn't mean that every missing tortoise was displaced by a burro.

It doesn't establish that burros are the sole cause of tortoise declines.

But it does tell us that the two were negatively associated across the study landscape.

And once again, water and vegetation are at the center of the story.

A 2024 study examining established burro herds in the Sonoran Desert found associations between burro presence and changes in several components of the plant community, including lower ground cover, plant density, foliage density, or plant size in several native species.[^6]

The effects weren't uniform across every species or every site.

The desert is complicated.

But that's exactly why concentrated use matters.

In a desert, recovery can be slow.

Water is scarce.

Vegetation is precious.

And animals that depend on the same limited resources have very little room for error.

## The Bill Comes Later

Ecological damage is difficult to put on an invoice.

Government spending isn't.

In fiscal year 2024, the BLM spent approximately **$153 million** on its Wild Horse and Burro Program.

About **$101 million—66 percent of total expenditures—went toward off-range holding**.[^1]

Think about that for a moment.

Two-thirds of the program's spending went toward caring for animals that had already been removed from public land.

And the animals still on the range were still reproducing.

When a horse is gathered, it doesn't simply disappear.

Someone has to feed it.

Someone has to provide veterinary care.

Someone has to transport it.

Someone has to maintain the pasture or corral where it lives.

If it isn't adopted or placed, those costs can continue for years.

The BLM's own data show how heavily the program has shifted toward maintaining animals already removed from the range.

In FY2024, off-range holding accounted for roughly two-thirds of program expenditures, compared with 5 percent for gathers and removals and 10 percent for adoptions.[^1]

It's a difficult cycle to escape.

More horses on the range create more pressure for gathers.

More gathers create more animals entering the off-range system.

More animals in the off-range system create more long-term costs.

And the more money that is consumed by animals already removed from the range, the harder it becomes to invest in preventing the next generation from entering the same system.

---

## What Are We Giving Up?

There is another way to think about the $153 million.

Not simply as money spent on horses.

But as money that cannot be spent somewhere else.

Riparian restoration.

Invasive species treatment.

Sage-grouse habitat.

Desert tortoise conservation.

Wildlife monitoring.

Spring restoration.

More field biologists.

More science.

The list goes on.

This is the **opportunity cost** of the current system, and it's one of the least visible parts of the debate.

There isn't an invoice for a failed sage-grouse nest.

There isn't a line item for a degraded spring.

There isn't a receipt for a meadow that no longer functions the way it once did.

But those costs are real.

They simply don't appear on the same spreadsheet.

---

## The Uncomfortable Middle

It's tempting to turn the wild horse debate into a simple choice.

Protect the horses.

Or protect the ecosystem.

But that's a false choice.

Horses are living animals.

They deserve humane treatment.

They are also large, non-native herbivores living in ecosystems that evolved without them.

Both things can be true.

We can love seeing horses on the landscape and still recognize that unlimited population growth isn't compatible with a healthy ecosystem.

We can reject cruel treatment without rejecting responsible population management.

And we can recognize that removing tens of thousands of animals from the range and placing them into long-term holding is not, by itself, a sustainable solution.

The harder questions are the ones we actually need to answer.

How many animals can a particular landscape support?

How should that number be determined?

How do we prevent populations from exceeding it?

What role should fertility control play?

When should animals be removed?

How should adoption and placement programs be structured?

And perhaps most importantly:

> **How do we manage horses as part of an ecosystem rather than treating them as an isolated issue?**

There aren't easy answers.

But avoiding the questions doesn't make the consequences disappear.

---

## The West Is Telling Us Something

Spend enough time on public lands and you begin to realize that landscapes rarely change all at once.

It happens gradually.

A trail becomes a road.

A meadow becomes bare ground.

A spring becomes a wallow.

A patch of habitat becomes fragmented.

A species becomes a little less common.

Then another.

Eventually, we look around and wonder how the landscape became so different.

Wild horses and burros are only one piece of that story.

Climate change, drought, wildfire, invasive species, livestock grazing, development, recreation, and habitat fragmentation all shape the future of Western ecosystems.

Acknowledging those other pressures doesn't make this one disappear.

The science is telling us that horse and burro populations substantially above ecological thresholds can have measurable consequences for vegetation, riparian systems, and native wildlife.

The financial data tells us that the current response is extraordinarily expensive.

And the landscapes themselves tell us something even simpler.

**They have limits.**

Maybe the goal shouldn't be to decide whether horses are good or bad.

Maybe the goal should be to build a system that allows these iconic animals to exist **without asking the ecosystems around them to absorb an impossible cost.**

Because public lands belong to more than the animals we find most beautiful.

They belong to the sage-grouse nesting in the sagebrush.

The tortoise crossing the desert.

The cottonwoods along a spring.

The grasses holding soil together.

The water moving through a riparian meadow.

And, ultimately, to all of us.

> **The question isn't whether wild horses belong in the West. It's whether we are willing to manage their populations in a way that allows the rest of the West to survive with them.**

## A Note on Terminology

We use "free-roaming horses and burros" throughout this article when discussing animals managed under the federal program.

Scientific literature commonly describes these populations as "feral" or "non-native" equids because modern populations descend from domesticated animals introduced to North America. Terminology surrounding these animals is highly contested, and we believe the ecological evidence is best discussed separately from that debate.
